# Christopher Pedregal

*Co-founder and CEO of Granola, the AI notepad for meetings. Previously founded Socratic, an AI tutor for high school students, acquired by Google. Source on shortening the feedback loop until it disappears, systematizing proximity to users, and why the right shape of a product is unknowable until it contacts the world.*

---

## Background

Christopher Pedregal co-founded and runs Granola, an AI notepad that listens during a meeting and turns whatever the user typed into finished notes afterward. The company launched roughly a year and a half before this note, at four people, and by the time of the interview had grown to thirty-five and raised a Series B at a $250 million valuation.

Before Granola he founded Socratic, an AI tutor for high school students, and ran it for five years in New York City until Google acquired it. He studied computer science and then human-computer interaction, and traces his entire practice back to a realization in a college security class: that almost every vulnerability came from systems not being designed in a way that made sense to people.

## Why he is on this wiki

Pedregal is a practitioner of the shortest possible loop between seeing a problem and shipping its fix, and he is unusually specific about the mechanics. He describes changing a confusing button in the middle of a session so the next person he shows it to teaches him the next problem instead of the same one. He describes paying high school students to spend afternoons in his office so that talking to a user cost nothing. He is equally clear about the discipline that keeps this from becoming order-taking: talk to users constantly, and do not build the list of things they ask for.

He also supplies the counterweight to fast building. Granola went unlaunched for a year, then cut half of what had been built once the real shape became clear, on the argument that polishing the wrong shape is throwing the work away.

## Principles contributed to this wiki

- **[Iterate at the Speed of Revelation](/principles/iterate-at-the-speed-of-revelation)**: sit beside the customer while they use the product, ship each fix in the moment that revealed it, and stop treating one user's confusion as something that needs nine more confirmations.

## Field Notes

### 2026-01-14 — "How to Build a Product that Hits PMF on Day 1" (EO, video interview)

A sixteen-minute interview that is mostly about loop latency. Lifted here: the one-person threshold for acting on a usability defect, the month-long feedback loop as worthless because the thinking behind the decision has already evaporated, proximity as something to systematize rather than schedule, the Socratic standing-room arrangement, the explore-then-exploit split with its warning about polishing the wrong shape, and the discipline of ignoring what users say they want while taking their context seriously.

> "You don't need to hear the same thing from 10 people. If I put a prototype in front of you and you say this button's super confusing, and I look at it and I'm like, oh I totally understand why you're confused, I don't need 10 other people to tell me that. I should go and I should change that button immediately, so that next time I show it to somebody I learn what the next problem is. And I think this is something that, especially in big companies, that's unheard of."
>
> — Christopher Pedregal, [How to Build a Product that Hits PMF on Day 1](https://youtu.be/hbGQaAXZ5UU), EO, 2026-01-14

> "If it takes a month to get feedback on it, it's almost not worth doing, because the thinking you had when you made the original decisions, you don't even remember."
>
> — Christopher Pedregal, 2026-01-14

> "The right solution is unknowable until you go out and you try it and it gets contact with the world. You can't sit and design the perfect thing. You need to put stuff out there, probe the system, and see how the system probes back."
>
> — Christopher Pedregal, 2026-01-14

> "You need to systematize your ability to talk to users. If the activation energy for you to go and talk to your user is high, then you're just not going to do it very often."
>
> — Christopher Pedregal, 2026-01-14

> "So I categorically ignore all positive things that people say."
>
> — Christopher Pedregal, 2026-01-14
