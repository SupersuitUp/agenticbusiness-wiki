# Pick an Easy Fight

*The fastest-growing companies are not the ones grinding hardest. They are the ones that chose an opponent they could beat: a massive, dusty, ancient industry, instead of the smartest and best-funded builders on the planet.*

## What it is

Which direction you build in is most of the game. Mallery interviewed the ten fastest-growing Y Combinator companies, from the hottest names in the current batch to the multi-billion-dollar alumni, and found that the winners share a category before they share any tactic:

> "Take some massive, dusty, ancient, old industry and rebuild it from the ground up with AI."
>
> — Finn Mallery, 2026-08-08

Corgi rebuilt insurance and went from zero to a multi-billion-dollar valuation in under two years. Legora rebuilt legal work, founded by two twenty-somethings from Sweden without law degrees, worth over $5 billion in under three years. Candid Health took medical billing: denied claims, insurance paperwork, actual fax machines. Arini took just the front reception desk of dental offices and grew nearly 200 percent in a year.

None of these are software for software people. That is the point.

## The losing default

The default instinct of talented engineers is to build for the world they know: another dev tool, another SaaS dashboard, another AI product for startups that already know how to use AI. That lane is a bloodbath. Every idea worth doing for software people has been attempted thousands of times, and the competition is the smartest, best-funded, most connected builders alive, working seven days a week.

> "The whole secret of this point is that you shouldn't compete with the best. That is a losing formula. You're making yourself in an unnecessarily difficult position."
>
> — Finn Mallery, 2026-08-08

The dusty industries invert every one of those conditions. Insurance is roughly 12 percent of GDP, about twice the size of the entire software industry. When Corgi walks into a building, its competition is a 60-year-old carrier still running on fax machines, and Corgi is the only modern company in the room.

## Why obscurity is an asset

The companies in this category are mostly names the tech world does not know, and that is a feature. The famous products for software people occupy enormous mindshare, which attracts more competitors every month. The industry rebuilds occupy almost none, which means the winners compound quietly until they are already massive incumbents. Mallery's read: the boring-industry cohort is growing faster than the famous cohort and has far bigger markets left to grow into.

## How to apply it

- **Size the industry, then look at its incumbents.** The target profile is a huge market whose dominant players are old, slow, and technologically decades behind. If the incumbents are sharp and modern, the fight is not easy.
- **Ignore the absence of domain credentials.** The founders in this cohort had no insurance licenses, no law degrees, no billing certifications. Domain expertise is manufacturable in months by getting close to the customer; [Get Pathologically Close to the Customer](/principles/pathologically-close-to-the-customer) is the mechanism.
- **Treat "boring and hated" as a signal, not a warning.** The more an industry repels talented builders, the fewer of them you will be competing against inside it.

The principle rhymes with [Niche Domination](/principles/niche-domination), and the two compose: the niche principle says pick a market the big fish cannot fit into, this one says pick a market the best fighters never enter. Both are ways of refusing the crowded fight. It is also the aggressive variant of the [Dinosaurs Versus Native](/principles/dinosaurs-versus-native) choice: this cohort picked the dinosaur game on purpose and brought native speed to it.
